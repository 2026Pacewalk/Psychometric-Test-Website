**Quick answer:** The most common career mistakes parents make are deciding *for* the child instead of *with* them, equating marks with aptitude, imposing their own dreams, comparing siblings or neighbours, and deciding under exam pressure. A psychometric assessment helps avoid all of them by basing decisions on the child's real strengths.

Every parent wants the best for their child. Yet some of the most damaging career decisions come from the most loving intentions. The problem isn't care — it's deciding without information.

At [TestPsychometric.com](/), operated by **AMG Educational Charitable Society**, we've seen these mistakes repeat across thousands of families. Here are the biggest ones — and how a [psychometric assessment](/blog/what-is-a-psychometric-test) helps you avoid each.

## Mistake 1: Deciding for the child instead of with them

When parents announce the decision ("you'll do engineering"), the child becomes a passenger in their own life. Even a good choice, imposed, kills motivation.

**The fix:** Decide *with* your child, using objective data both of you can see. A psychometric report gives you a shared, neutral starting point — the child owns the choice within informed options.

## Mistake 2: Treating marks as the career compass

"She's good at Maths, so engineering." But marks measure exam performance on one syllabus — not interest, personality, or real aptitude. Plenty of high scorers are miserable in the careers their marks "pointed to."

**The fix:** Separate marks from fit. A [psychometric test measures aptitude, interest and personality](/blog/aptitude-test-vs-psychometric-test) — the things that actually predict career success.

## Mistake 3: Imposing your own unfulfilled dreams

"I couldn't become a doctor, so my child will." This turns a child's future into a parent's second chance — rarely a happy outcome.

**The fix:** Notice the difference between *your* dream and *your child's* fit. If the data shows your child suits medicine, wonderful. If not, supporting their real strengths is the greater gift.

## Mistake 4: Comparing your child to others

"Your cousin got into IIT, why can't you?" Comparisons create pressure and push children toward paths chosen for someone else's brain.

**The fix:** Every child has a unique profile. A psychometric report makes that uniqueness visible, so you guide *your* child, not a copy of someone else's.

## Mistake 5: Believing some streams are "useless"

"Arts has no scope." This outdated belief blocks children from law, civil services, psychology, design, and media — fields with enormous scope.

**The fix:** Judge streams by fit, not reputation. Read [Science, Commerce or Arts?](/blog/science-commerce-or-arts) for an honest look at each.

## Mistake 6: Deciding under last-minute pressure

Leaving the decision until board results arrive adds panic to an already hard choice. Rushed decisions are rarely good ones.

**The fix:** Assess early — Class 9 or 10 — so the family has time to explore calmly. See [career guidance after 10th](/blog/career-guidance-after-10th).

## Mistake 7: Ignoring the child's emotional signals

A child dreading a subject, losing confidence, or going quiet is sending a message. Parents focused only on marks often miss it.

**The fix:** A psychometric report includes personality and self-awareness indicators, surfacing what your child may struggle to say out loud.

Notice the pattern: every mistake comes from deciding *without data about the child*. A psychometric assessment fixes the root cause by giving you:

- Recommended streams and career clusters that fit

## A real example

A father in Amritsar was determined his son would become an engineer. The boy was failing to keep up and losing confidence. The assessment revealed strong **artistic** and **spatial** intelligence — a designer's profile, not an engineer's. The father, to his credit, listened. ## Expert advice for parents

- **Be a coach, not a commander.** Guide with questions and data, not orders.
- **Lead with strengths.** Start every career talk from what your child is good at.
- **Use a counsellor.** Pair the report with a conversation — read our [parent's guide to counselling](/blog/career-counselling-parents-guide).
- **Stay flexible.** Careers evolve; a good start matters more than a perfect prediction.
